Research framing

My lab work sits in the gap between a learned locomotion policy and the machine that must execute it. I treat each build, training run, and deployment attempt as a traceable experiment: what command was sent, what the actuator actually did, what the logs recorded, and which mechanical or control assumption failed.

Built the hardware-control interface that robot learning depends on.

In ASR Lab, I worked close to the actuator layer: cycloidal and planetary reduction gearbox concepts, fabrication details, motor command/readback routines, control-board programming, wiring checks, calibration, and repeated hardware repair. The work made one point very clear: robot learning only becomes useful when the physical platform can execute commands repeatably within actuator, gearbox, and sensing limits.

I treated each motor module as both a mechanism and a measurement device. Rough rotation, high current, encoder drift, or violent initial motion could not be explained from software alone, so I traced failures through reducer assembly, magnet-seat tolerance, ODrive settings, motor ID/communication checks, wiring, and real joint behavior.

My hardware notes include Cubemars RO80 motor testing, current and velocity-limit behavior, encoder and magnet-seat tolerance issues, calf output shaft failures, coaxiality concerns, material choices, and assembly revisions made after physical tests. That record helped turn broken parts and unstable motion into usable design requirements for the next build.

Treated locomotion training as an engineering experiment, not a black box.

I recorded and compared locomotion training runs in Isaac Lab with the same mindset I use for hardware tests: isolate one hypothesis, compare the evidence, then decide whether the next change belongs in reward design, command settings, observation choices, or the physical robot assumption. The logs covered foot clearance, flat orientation, gait timing, base height, contact penalties, torque limits, action-rate penalties, and stand-still behavior.

The important skill was not just launching training. It was learning what a policy was optimizing, why a gait became unstable or too rigid, when imitation learning helped produce natural motion, and when it limited command-following flexibility. I used checkpoints, TensorBoard curves, simulation videos, and test notes to make the training process readable enough to support engineering decisions.

Closed the sim-to-real loop through logs, rosbag data, and failure analysis.

Deployment work forced the system view: launch motor and sensor communication, verify the standing pose, run the learned policy, record rosbag data, inspect command and motor topics, compare simulated foot-height outputs with physical behavior, then decide whether the next fix belonged in hardware, calibration, state estimation, communication, or policy design.

I helped analyze issues such as violent initial motor motion, standing-pose mismatch, motor protection, joint shaking, front-foot dragging, IMU unit interpretation, Teensy to ODrive communication faults, loose shaft links, and repeated calf shaft failures. Logs, topic data, run comparisons, and physical inspection helped separate policy problems from hardware and communication problems, so failures became design evidence instead of noise.

This loop strengthened the kind of judgment I want to bring to graduate robotics research: do not trust a clean simulation until the actuator response, sensing pipeline, communication timing, and physical failure history explain why the real robot should behave the same way.

Engineering flow

01 Build the actuator layer

Assemble reducers, motor modules, shafts, wiring, control boards, encoder seats, and motor IDs.

02 Make control measurable

Check command/readback behavior, current limits, velocity limits, ODrive setup, and board communication.

03 Train and evaluate policies

Use Isaac Lab RL/IL workflows, reward shaping, checkpoints, TensorBoard records, and simulation videos.

04 Deploy, log, and revise

Run ROS2 launches and rosbag checks, then trace failures back to hardware, communication, calibration, or policy design.

Reviewer takeaway

ASR Lab is the strongest signal of my current research direction: I am not only interested in robot learning as an algorithmic problem, but in the complete physical loop that makes learning-based locomotion work on real hardware. I can move between reducer design, embedded motor control, training analysis, ROS2 deployment, and failure investigation without treating them as separate worlds. The experience strengthens my fit for graduate robotics research in legged robots, actuator design, motor-control interfaces, and sim-to-real experimentation.
